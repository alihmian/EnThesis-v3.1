\chapter{Topological Dynamics and Universal Minimal Flows}
\label{chap:topological-dynamics}

The automorphism groups considered in this thesis carry natural topologies, and
the Kechris--Pestov--Todorcevic correspondence studies their continuous actions
on compact spaces.  This chapter collects precisely the topological-group and
dynamical background needed for that purpose.  We first record the elementary
facts about translations, subgroups, coset spaces, and quotient groups that will
be used later.  We then introduce flows, minimality, the universal minimal flow,
extreme amenability, and ambits.

\section{A topological-group toolbox}

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{topological group} is a group \(G\) equipped with a topology for
    which multiplication \(G\times G\to G\), \((g,h)\mapsto gh\), and inversion
    \(G\to G\), \(g\mapsto g^{-1}\), are continuous.
\end{definition}

The compatibility of the topology with the group operations makes the topology
of \(G\) homogeneous.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:translations-homeomorphisms}
    Let \(G\) be a topological group and let \(a,b\in G\).  Inversion, left and
    right translation, and conjugation are homeomorphisms.  More precisely, the
    maps
    \[
        x\mapsto x^{-1},\qquad x\mapsto ax,\qquad x\mapsto xb,\qquad
        x\mapsto axb,\qquad x\mapsto axa^{-1}
    \]
    are homeomorphisms of \(G\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Inversion is continuous and is its own inverse.  The map \(x\mapsto ax\) is
    continuous by continuity of multiplication, and its inverse is
    \(x\mapsto a^{-1}x\); the argument for right translation is identical.  The
    remaining maps are compositions of these homeomorphisms.
\end{proof}

If \(H\leq G\), then \(H\), with the subspace topology, is a topological group:
its operations are restrictions of the operations of \(G\).  Coset spaces will
also occur repeatedly.  For a subgroup \(H\leq G\), let
\(G/H=\{gH:g\in G\}\), let \(\pi_H(g)=gH\), and give \(G/H\) the quotient
topology induced by \(\pi_H\).

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:quotient-map-open}
    For every subgroup \(H\leq G\), the quotient map
    \(\pi_H\colon G\to G/H\) is continuous, surjective, and open.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Continuity and surjectivity follow from the definition of the quotient
    topology.  If \(U\subseteq G\) is open, then
    \(\pi_H^{-1}(\pi_H(U))=UH=\bigcup_{h\in H}Uh\).  Every right translate
    \(Uh\) is open by Proposition~\ref{prop:translations-homeomorphisms};
    hence \(UH\) is open, and the definition of the quotient topology shows
    that \(\pi_H(U)\) is open.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    \label{cor:open-subgroup-discrete-cosets}
    If \(H\leq G\) is open, then \(G/H\) is discrete.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    The singleton \(\{H\}=\pi_H(H)\) is open by
    Proposition~\ref{prop:quotient-map-open}.  Every other singleton is a left
    translate of \(\{H\}\), and the natural action of \(G\) on \(G/H\) is by
    homeomorphisms.  Thus every singleton is open.
\end{proof}

When \(N\trianglelefteq G\) is normal, the coset space has the quotient-group
operations \((gN)(hN)=ghN\) and \((gN)^{-1}=g^{-1}N\).

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:quotient-group}
    Let \(G\) be a topological group and \(N\trianglelefteq G\).  Then \(G/N\),
    equipped with the quotient topology, is a topological group.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(q\colon G\to G/N\) be the quotient map.  The algebraic operations are
    well defined because \(N\) is normal.  The map \(q\) is open by
    Proposition~\ref{prop:quotient-map-open}, so \(q\times q\) is an open
    continuous surjection and therefore a quotient map.  Multiplication on
    \(G/N\) is the unique map \(\widetilde m\) satisfying
    \(\widetilde m\circ(q\times q)=q\circ m\), where \(m\) is multiplication
    on \(G\).  Since \(q\circ m\) is continuous, the quotient property gives
    continuity of \(\widetilde m\).  Similarly, inversion on \(G/N\) is the
    unique map \(\widetilde i\) satisfying \(\widetilde i\circ q=q\circ i\);
    hence it is continuous.
\end{proof}

Closedness of the normal subgroup gives the separation property needed in most
applications.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:closed-normal-hausdorff-quotient}
    Let \(G\) be a Hausdorff topological group and let
    \(N\trianglelefteq G\) be closed.  Then \(G/N\) is Hausdorff.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose \(gN\neq hN\).  Then \(h^{-1}g\notin N\).  Because \(N\) is closed,
    choose an open neighborhood \(U\) of \(h^{-1}g\) disjoint from \(N\).
    Continuity of \((x,y)\mapsto y^{-1}x\) gives neighborhoods \(V\) of \(g\)
    and \(W\) of \(h\) such that \(W^{-1}V\subseteq U\).  The sets \(q(V)\)
    and \(q(W)\) are open neighborhoods of \(gN\) and \(hN\), respectively,
    because \(q\) is open.  If they met, some \(v\in V\) and \(w\in W\) would
    satisfy \(vN=wN\), whence \(w^{-1}v\in N\).  This contradicts
    \(w^{-1}v\in U\).  Thus the two cosets have disjoint neighborhoods.
\end{proof}

In particular, open subgroups yield discrete \(G\)-spaces \(G/H\), while closed
normal subgroups yield Hausdorff topological quotient groups.  These are the
only general quotient facts needed below.

\section{Flows and subflows}

\begin{definition}
    Let \(G\) be a topological group.  A \emph{\(G\)-flow} is a compact
    Hausdorff space \(X\) together with a continuous action
    \(G\times X\to X\), \((g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x\).
\end{definition}

For \(x\in X\), its orbit is \(G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}\).  A subset
\(Y\subseteq X\) is \emph{\(G\)-invariant} if \(g\cdot Y=Y\) for every
\(g\in G\).  A \emph{subflow} of \(X\) is a nonempty closed invariant subset,
equipped with the restricted action.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:orbit-closure-subflow}
    If \(X\) is a \(G\)-flow and \(x\in X\), then
    \(\overline{G\cdot x}\) is a subflow of \(X\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The orbit closure is nonempty and closed.  For any \(g\in G\), the action
    of \(g\) on \(X\) is a homeomorphism, and
    \(g\cdot(G\cdot x)=G\cdot x\).  Consequently,
    \(g\cdot\overline{G\cdot x}=\overline{G\cdot x}\), so the orbit closure is
    invariant.
\end{proof}

\section{Minimal flows}

\begin{definition}
    A \(G\)-flow \(X\) is \emph{minimal} if it has no proper subflow.
\end{definition}

Minimality says that the action cannot be restricted to a smaller nonempty
closed invariant space.  It has the following useful orbit formulation.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:minimal-dense-orbits}
    A \(G\)-flow \(X\) is minimal if and only if every orbit in \(X\) is dense.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    If \(X\) is minimal and \(x\in X\), then
    Proposition~\ref{prop:orbit-closure-subflow} makes
    \(\overline{G\cdot x}\) a subflow, so it equals \(X\).  Conversely, suppose
    every orbit is dense and let \(Y\) be a subflow.  For \(y\in Y\),
    invariance and closedness give
    \(X=\overline{G\cdot y}\subseteq Y\), and therefore \(Y=X\).
\end{proof}

Compactness guarantees that every flow contains a minimal part.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:existence-minimal-subflow}
    Every \(G\)-flow contains a minimal subflow.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(\mathcal S\) be the family of nonempty closed invariant subsets of
    \(X\), ordered by inclusion.  It is nonempty because \(X\in\mathcal S\).
    The intersection of any descending chain in \(\mathcal S\) is closed and
    invariant.  It is also nonempty: the chain has the finite intersection
    property, and \(X\) is compact.  Zorn's lemma, applied with the reverse
    order, therefore gives an inclusion-minimal member \(M\) of \(\mathcal S\).
    By definition, \(M\) is a minimal subflow.
\end{proof}

\section{Universal minimal flows}

The relevant maps between flows are the continuous equivariant ones.

\begin{definition}
    If \(X\) and \(Y\) are \(G\)-flows, a \emph{flow morphism} is a continuous
    map \(\pi\colon X\to Y\) satisfying
    \(\pi(g\cdot x)=g\cdot\pi(x)\) for all \(g\in G\) and \(x\in X\).
    When such a map is surjective, \(Y\) is called a \emph{factor} of \(X\).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:hom-to-minimal-surjective}
    A flow morphism from a nonempty \(G\)-flow to a minimal target is
    automatically surjective.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    If \(\pi\colon X\to Y\) is a flow morphism and \(Y\) is minimal, then
    \(\pi(X)\) is nonempty and invariant.  It is compact and hence closed in
    the Hausdorff space \(Y\).  Thus \(\pi(X)\) is a subflow of \(Y\), so
    minimality gives \(\pi(X)=Y\).
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{universal minimal flow} of \(G\) is a minimal \(G\)-flow \(M\)
    such that every minimal \(G\)-flow is a factor of \(M\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:universal-minimal-flow}
    Every topological group \(G\) has a universal minimal flow, unique up to
    isomorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
    A standard cardinality bound permits one to choose a set
    \(\{X_i:i\in I\}\) containing a representative of every isomorphism class
    of minimal \(G\)-flows.  Give \(P=\prod_{i\in I}X_i\) the coordinatewise
    action.  By compactness \(P\) is a \(G\)-flow, and by
    Proposition~\ref{prop:existence-minimal-subflow} it contains a minimal
    subflow \(M\).  For each \(i\), the restriction to \(M\) of the coordinate
    projection \(P\to X_i\) is a flow morphism.  It is surjective by
    Proposition~\ref{prop:hom-to-minimal-surjective}, so every minimal flow is
    a factor of \(M\).

    For uniqueness, one uses the standard coalescence property of a universal
    minimal flow: every endomorphism of such a flow is an automorphism.  If
    \(M\) and \(N\) are universal minimal flows, universality supplies morphisms
    \(M\to N\) and \(N\to M\).  Their composites are endomorphisms and hence
    automorphisms; it follows that the original morphisms are isomorphisms.
    These standard existence and coalescence arguments may be found in
    \cite{A,dV}.
\end{proof}

We denote the universal minimal flow by \(M(G)\).  It is the canonical maximal
object among minimal flows in the factor order, and computing it is one of the
main objectives of the later chapters.

\section{Extreme amenability}

\begin{definition}
    A point \(x\) of a \(G\)-flow is \emph{fixed} if \(g\cdot x=x\) for every
    \(g\in G\).  A topological group \(G\) is \emph{extremely amenable} if every
    \(G\)-flow has a fixed point.
\end{definition}

The universal minimal flow packages this fixed-point property into a single
object.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:extreme-amenable-iff-umf-singleton}
    A topological group \(G\) is extremely amenable if and only if \(M(G)\) is
    a singleton.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    If \(G\) is extremely amenable, \(M(G)\) has a fixed point \(x\).  The
    singleton \(\{x\}\) is a subflow, so minimality gives \(M(G)=\{x\}\).

    Conversely, suppose \(M(G)\) is a singleton and let \(X\) be any
    \(G\)-flow.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:existence-minimal-subflow}, \(X\)
    contains a minimal subflow \(Y\).  Universality gives a morphism
    \(M(G)\to Y\), which is surjective by
    Proposition~\ref{prop:hom-to-minimal-surjective}.  Hence \(Y\) is a
    singleton, and its unique point is fixed in \(X\).
\end{proof}

Extreme amenability is the dynamical property that the structural Ramsey
results of this thesis will detect for automorphism groups.

\section{Ambits}

\begin{definition}
    A \emph{\(G\)-ambit} is a pair \((X,x_0)\), where \(X\) is a \(G\)-flow and
    the distinguished point \(x_0\) has dense orbit.  A morphism of ambits
    \(\pi\colon(X,x_0)\to(Y,y_0)\) is a flow morphism satisfying
    \(\pi(x_0)=y_0\).
\end{definition}

Thus an ambit records not merely a flow with a dense orbit, but also a chosen
point whose orbit is dense.  This extra datum forces rigidity of morphisms.

\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:ambit-hom-unique}
    Between two \(G\)-ambits there is at most one ambit morphism.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(\pi_1,\pi_2\colon(X,x_0)\to(Y,y_0)\) be ambit morphisms.  For every
    \(g\in G\),
    \(\pi_1(g\cdot x_0)=g\cdot y_0=\pi_2(g\cdot x_0)\).  Hence the maps agree
    on the dense set \(G\cdot x_0\).  Since \(Y\) is Hausdorff and both maps
    are continuous, they agree on all of \(X\).
\end{proof}

Ambits will be useful when a compact dynamical space is obtained as the closure
of a concrete orbit.  The concepts developed in this chapter now provide the
topological and dynamical framework for relating Fra\"iss\'e expansions and
Ramsey properties to extreme amenability and universal minimal flows.
